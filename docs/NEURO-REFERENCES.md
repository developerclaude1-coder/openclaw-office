# NEURO-REFERENCES — the biology citation register

Companion to [NEURO-MAPPING.md](./NEURO-MAPPING.md). Every entry was verified against PubMed (existence,
year, journal) by the research pass, and ~50 abstracts carrying quantitative or attributional claims were
re-fetched by a verification critic. Each entry records the specific claim it supports in this project —
a reference that resolves but supports nothing is decoration, and was excluded. Corrections and contested
claims are listed in NEURO-MAPPING.md § Citation integrity and repeated in the final section here.

## Neurogenesis and neuronal migration

- Doetsch F, Caillé I, Lim DA, García-Verdugo JM, Alvarez-Buylla A (1999). **Subventricular zone astrocytes are neural stem cells in the adult mammalian brain** *Cell*. PMID:10380923.
  Supports: GFAP+ astrocyte-like B1 cells of the adult SVZ are the neural stem cells that generate transit-amplifying precursors, neuroblasts, and ultimately new olfactory bulb neurons.
- Noctor SC, Flint AC, Weissman TA, Dammerman RS, Kriegstein AR (2001). **Neurons derived from radial glial cells establish radial units in neocortex** *Nature*. PMID:11217860.
  Supports: Radial glia are neuronal progenitors in vivo, and newborn neurons migrate along the radial fibers of their own clonally related radial glia, making the lineage parent also the migration scaffold (radial unit).
- Rakic P (1972). **Mode of cell migration to the superficial layers of fetal monkey neocortex** *Journal of Comparative Neurology*. PMID:4624784.
  Supports: Classic EM study establishing that migrating cortical neurons are guided along radial glial fibers from the ventricular zone to their cortical destination (glia-guided locomotion).
- Lois C, Alvarez-Buylla A (1994). **Long-distance neuronal migration in the adult mammalian brain** *Science*. PMID:8178174.
  Supports: Adult SVZ cells migrate long distances (the rostral migratory stream route) and differentiate into olfactory bulb neurons, demonstrating targeted long-range neuroblast migration in the adult brain.
- Eriksson PS, Perfilieva E, Björk-Eriksson T, Alborn AM, Nordborg C, Peterson DA, Gage FH (1998). **Neurogenesis in the adult human hippocampus** *Nature Medicine*. PMID:9809557.
  Supports: BrdU-labeled dividing progenitors generate new NeuN+ neurons in the adult human dentate gyrus, the foundational evidence for lifelong human hippocampal neurogenesis.
- Ming GL, Song H (2011). **Adult neurogenesis in the mammalian brain: significant answers and significant questions** *Neuron*. PMID:21609825.
  Supports: Modern review consolidating the SGZ and SVZ->RMS->olfactory bulb pipelines, the stem cell -> amplifying progenitor -> neuroblast -> mature neuron stages, niche regulation, and integration timelines.
- Ge S, Goh EL, Sailor KA, Kitabatake Y, Ming GL, Song H (2006). **GABA regulates synaptic integration of newly generated neurons in the adult brain** *Nature*. PMID:16341203.
  Supports: Newborn granule cells are first tonically activated by ambient GABA, which is depolarizing due to high intracellular chloride; integration follows a fixed GABA-then-glutamate sequence, and converting GABA's action to hyperpolarizing early causes marked defects in synapse formation and dendritic development.
- Ge S, Yang CH, Hsu KS, Ming GL, Song H (2007). **A critical period for enhanced synaptic plasticity in newly generated neurons of the adult brain** *Neuron*. PMID:17521569.
  Supports: Adult-born granule cells at 1-1.5 months of age show enhanced LTP amplitude and lowered induction threshold, dependent on NR2B-containing NMDA receptors — a transient critical period of elevated plasticity.
- Tashiro A, Sandler VM, Toni N, Zhao C, Gage FH (2006). **NMDA-receptor-mediated, cell-specific integration of new neurons in adult dentate gyrus** *Nature*. PMID:16906136.
  Supports: Single-cell NMDA receptor knockout shows survival of new neurons is competitively regulated by each cell's own input activity during a short critical period after birth — the input-gated, use-it-or-lose-it survival checkpoint.
- Sun W, Winseck A, Vinsant S, Park OH, Kim H, Oppenheim RW (2004). **Programmed cell death of adult-generated hippocampal neurons is mediated by the proapoptotic gene Bax** *Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:15590937.
  Supports: The programmed death of adult-generated dentate neurons is executed by Bax; Bax deletion abolishes this death and granule neurons accumulate, showing apoptosis is the default fate operating during early differentiation.
- Sierra A, Encinas JM, Deudero JJ, Chancey JH, Enikolopov G, Overstreet-Wadiche LS, Tsirka SE, Maletic-Savatic M (2010). **Microglia shape adult hippocampal neurogenesis through apoptosis-coupled phagocytosis** *Cell Stem Cell*. PMID:20887954.
  Supports: The majority of newborn SGZ cells die by apoptosis in days 1-4 of life (amplifying progenitor to neuroblast transition) and are rapidly and efficiently cleared by unchallenged resident microglia.
- Kempermann G, Kuhn HG, Gage FH (1997). **More hippocampal neurons in adult mice living in an enriched environment** *Nature*. PMID:9087407.
  Supports: Environmental enrichment increases the number of surviving new dentate granule neurons, demonstrating experience-dependent regulation of newborn neuron survival.
- Toni N, Laplagne DA, Zhao C, Lombardi G, Ribak CE, Gage FH, Schinder AF (2008). **Neurons born in the adult dentate gyrus form functional synapses with target cells** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:18622400.
  Supports: Adult-born granule cells form functional glutamatergic output synapses onto hilar interneurons, mossy cells, and CA3 pyramidal cells, completing bidirectional integration into the pre-existing circuit.
- Clelland CD, Choi M, Romberg C, Clemenson GD, Fragniere A, Tyers P, Jessberger S, Saksida LM, Barker RA, Gage FH, Bussey TJ (2009). **A functional role for adult hippocampal neurogenesis in spatial pattern separation** *Science*. PMID:19590004.
  Supports: Ablating adult hippocampal neurogenesis selectively impairs discrimination of closely spaced (highly similar) spatial stimuli, showing newborn neurons are required for fine pattern separation in the dentate gyrus.
- Akers KG, Martinez-Canabal A, Restivo L, et al., Frankland PW (2014). **Hippocampal neurogenesis regulates forgetting during adulthood and infancy** *Science*. PMID:24812394.
  Supports: Increasing neurogenesis after memory formation induces forgetting of established hippocampal memories; high infant neurogenesis underlies infantile amnesia, and suppressing it mitigates forgetting — continuous unit addition actively remodels and degrades stored circuits.
- Sorrells SF, Paredes MF, Cebrian-Silla A, et al., Alvarez-Buylla A (2018). **Human hippocampal neurogenesis drops sharply in children to undetectable levels in adults** *Nature*. PMID:29513649.
  Supports: In carefully staged human post-mortem and surgical tissue, proliferating dentate progenitors and young neurons decline sharply in childhood and are undetectable in adults — the strongest evidence that adult human hippocampal neurogenesis is rare or absent.
- Boldrini M, Fulmore CA, Tartt AN, et al., Mann JJ (2018). **Human Hippocampal Neurogenesis Persists throughout Aging** *Cell Stem Cell*. PMID:29625071.
  Supports: In healthy human autopsy hippocampi aged 14-79, similar numbers of intermediate progenitors and thousands of immature neurons are found across ages — the counter-evidence that human hippocampal neurogenesis persists lifelong (with reduced quiescent pool and angiogenesis in aging).

## Growth cones, filopodia and axon guidance

- Lowery LA, Van Vactor D (2009). **The trip of the tip: understanding the growth cone machinery** *Nature Reviews Molecular Cell Biology*. PMID:19373241.
  Supports: Review of growth cone architecture: the cytoskeletal 'vehicle' (actin treadmill, retrograde flow, clutch-based traction) and the 'navigator' that translates environmental signals into spatially biased, directional movement.
- Mitchison T, Kirschner M (1988). **Cytoskeletal dynamics and nerve growth** *Neuron*. PMID:3078414.
  Supports: Foundational statement of the molecular clutch hypothesis: growth cone advance results from coupling retrograde actin flow to the substrate via adhesion, converting treadmilling into traction.
- Bentley D, Toroian-Raymond A (1986). **Disoriented pathfinding by pioneer neurone growth cones deprived of filopodia by cytochalasin treatment** *Nature*. PMID:3773996.
  Supports: In vivo demonstration that filopodia are dispensable for axon elongation but required for steering: cytochalasin-treated Ti1 pioneer growth cones without filopodia keep extending yet become highly disoriented.
- Serafini T, Kennedy TE, Galko MJ, Mirzayan C, Jessell TM, Tessier-Lavigne M (1994). **The netrins define a family of axon outgrowth-promoting proteins homologous to C. elegans UNC-6** *Cell*. PMID:8062384.
  Supports: Purification and cloning of netrin-1/netrin-2 as diffusible floor-plate-derived promoters of commissural axon outgrowth, homologous to C. elegans UNC-6 — the founding evidence for conserved diffusible chemoattractant gradients.
- Luo Y, Raible D, Raper JA (1993). **Collapsin: a protein in brain that induces the collapse and paralysis of neuronal growth cones** *Cell*. PMID:8402908.
  Supports: Identification of collapsin (Sema3A): effective at ~10 pM, it collapses sensory but not retinal growth cones, establishing receptor-selective repulsive guidance by motility inhibition.
- Song H, Ming G, He Z, Lehmann M, McKerracher L, Tessier-Lavigne M, Poo M (1998). **Conversion of neuronal growth cone responses from repulsion to attraction by cyclic nucleotides** *Science*. PMID:9727979.
  Supports: Pharmacological elevation of cGMP or cAMP signaling converts Sema3A- and MAG-induced repulsive turning into attraction — cue valence is set by the growth cone's internal second-messenger state, not by the ligand.
- Drescher U, Kremoser C, Handwerker C, Loschinger J, Noda M, Bonhoeffer F (1995). **In vitro guidance of retinal ganglion cell axons by RAGS, a 25 kDa tectal protein related to ligands for Eph receptor tyrosine kinases** *Cell*. PMID:7634326.
  Supports: Identification of RAGS (ephrin-A2), a GPI-anchored Eph-receptor ligand expressed in a gradient in posterior tectum that collapses and repels retinal axons — the molecular basis of graded topographic map formation.
- Rosoff WJ, Urbach JS, Esrick MA, McAllister RG, Richards LJ, Goodhill GJ (2004). **A new chemotaxis assay shows the extreme sensitivity of axons to molecular gradients** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:15162167.
  Supports: Quantitative 3D gradient assay showing axons are extraordinarily sensitive gradient detectors (fractional differences of order 0.1% across the growth cone), but only within a narrow absolute concentration range — implying adaptation/gain control.
- Kolodkin AL, Tessier-Lavigne M (2011). **Mechanisms and molecules of neuronal wiring: a primer** *Cold Spring Harbor Perspectives in Biology*. PMID:21123392.
  Supports: Modern authoritative review of the guidance-cue lexicon (netrins, semaphorins, ephrins, slits and their receptors), the four guidance modes (contact/chemo x attraction/repulsion), intermediate targets, and signal transduction to cytoskeletal steering.
- Ziv NE, Smith SJ (1996). **Evidence for a role of dendritic filopodia in synaptogenesis and spine formation** *Neuron*. PMID:8755481.
  Supports: Live imaging showing transient dendritic filopodia (mean lifetime 9.5 min, up to 10 um long) actively initiate contacts with axons within 5-10 um; stabilized contacts acquire functional presynaptic boutons and evolve into dendritic spines.
- Lohmann C, Bonhoeffer T (2008). **A role for local calcium signaling in rapid synaptic partner selection by dendritic filopodia** *Neuron*. PMID:18667153.
  Supports: Filopodium-axon contacts evoke local dendritic calcium transients within seconds; high transient frequency predicts contact stabilization, low frequency predicts retraction, and contacts with a certain GABAergic axon population are never stabilized — partner selection precedes mature synapse formation.
- Niell CM, Meyer MP, Smith SJ (2004). **In vivo imaging of synapse formation on a growing dendritic arbor** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:14758365.
  Supports: In live zebrafish tectum, nearly all nascent synapses form on newly extended dendritic filopodia; maintained synapses stabilize their host filopodium into a branch, supporting the synaptotropic model in which validated synapses direct arbor growth.

## Synaptogenesis

- Ziv NE, Smith SJ (1996). **Evidence for a role of dendritic filopodia in synaptogenesis and spine formation** *Neuron*. PMID:8755481 / doi:10.1016/s0896-6273(00)80283-4.
  Supports: Dendritic filopodia up to 10 µm long with mean lifetime ~9.5 min transiently probe a 5–10 µm neighbourhood, initiate contact with nearby axons, and a subset stabilize into spines bearing functional presynaptic boutons — the exploratory search mechanism and its ~10-minute sampling window.
- Niell CM, Meyer MP, Smith SJ (2004). **In vivo imaging of synapse formation on a growing dendritic arbor** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:14758365 / doi:10.1038/nn1191.
  Supports: Almost all synapses form on newly extended dendritic filopodia in vivo; maintenance of the nascent synapse is what stabilizes the filopodium, which then matures into a branch (synaptotropic growth) — validation of a probe drives structural commitment.
- Ahmari SE, Buchanan J, Smith SJ (2000). **Assembly of presynaptic active zones from cytoplasmic transport packets** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:10769383 / doi:10.1038/74814.
  Supports: Presynaptic active-zone precursors travel the axon as discrete pre-packaged transport packets that are stabilized specifically at new sites of contact, with evoked vesicle recycling observable within less than one hour — assembly is capture of prefabricated modules, not local synthesis.
- Friedman HV, Bresler T, Garner CC, Ziv NE (2000). **Assembly of new individual excitatory synapses: time course and temporal order of synaptic molecule recruitment** *Neuron*. PMID:10939331 / doi:10.1016/s0896-6273(00)00009-x.
  Supports: Functional presynaptic boutons with Bassoon form within 30 min of initial axodendritic contact; PSD-95 and AMPA/NMDA receptor clusters appear on average ~45 min later; complete glutamatergic synapse assembly takes 1–2 h with presynaptic preceding postsynaptic differentiation.
- Scheiffele P, Fan J, Choih J, Fetter R, Serafini T (2000). **Neuroligin expressed in nonneuronal cells triggers presynaptic development in contacting axons** *Cell*. PMID:10892652 / doi:10.1016/s0092-8674(00)80877-6.
  Supports: Non-neuronal cells engineered to express neuroligin-1/2 induce de novo morphological and functional presynaptic differentiation in contacting axons, and this is blocked by soluble beta-neurexin decoy — postsynaptic adhesion molecules alone are a sufficient instructive induction signal.
- Graf ER, Zhang X, Jin SX, Linhoff MW, Craig AM (2004). **Neurexins induce differentiation of GABA and glutamate postsynaptic specializations via neuroligins** *Cell*. PMID:15620359 / doi:10.1016/j.cell.2004.11.035.
  Supports: Induction is bidirectional (neurexin alone induces postsynaptic differentiation; neuroligins induce presynaptic differentiation) and isoform identity carries the type code — neuroligin-2 localizes to and specifies GABAergic sites while neuroligins-1/3/4 specify glutamatergic sites.
- Lee SJ, Uemura T, Yoshida T, Mishina M (2012). **GluRδ2 assembles four neurexins into trans-synaptic triad to trigger synapse formation** *The Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:22457515 / doi:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.5584-11.2012.
  Supports: The synaptogenic triad assembles from tetrameric GluRδ2, hexameric Cbln1 and monomeric neurexin in a 1:2:4 ratio; dimeric GluRδ2-NTD plus Cbln1 fails to accumulate presynaptic Bassoon/VGLUT1 while tetramerized GluRδ2-NTD succeeds — induction has a clustering/valency (quorum) threshold on engaged partner count.
- Varoqueaux F, Aramuni G, Rawson RL, Mohrmann R, Missler M, Gottmann K, Zhang W, Südhof TC, Brose N (2006). **Neuroligins determine synapse maturation and function** *Neuron*. PMID:16982420 / doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2006.09.003.
  Supports: Neuroligin-deficient mice show unaltered density of synaptic contacts but reduced glutamatergic and GABAergic/glycinergic transmission and fatal neonatal respiratory failure — formation of a contact and its maturation/validation are mechanistically dissociable steps.
- Chubykin AA, Atasoy D, Etherton MR, Brose N, Kavalali ET, Gibson JR, Südhof TC (2007). **Activity-dependent validation of excitatory versus inhibitory synapses by neuroligin-1 versus neuroligin-2** *Neuron*. PMID:17582332 / doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2007.05.029.
  Supports: Chronic NMDA-receptor or CaMKII inhibition suppresses neuroligin-1's synapse-boosting activity while chronic general activity blockade suppresses neuroligin-2's — neuroligins do not establish but specify and validate synapses through a class-specific activity-dependent gate.
- Rakic P, Bourgeois JP, Eckenhoff MF, Zecevic N, Goldman-Rakic PS (1986). **Concurrent overproduction of synapses in diverse regions of the primate cerebral cortex** *Science*. PMID:3952506 / doi:10.1126/science.3952506.
  Supports: Synapses develop concurrently and at identical rates across visual, somatosensory, motor and prefrontal primate cortex, indicating a globally clocked, non-hierarchical overproduction phase rather than per-region demand-driven construction.
- Buffelli M, Burgess RW, Feng G, Lobe CG, Lichtman JW, Sanes JR (2003). **Genetic evidence that relative synaptic efficacy biases the outcome of synaptic competition** *Nature*. PMID:12879071 / doi:10.1038/nature01844.
  Supports: Selectively inhibiting neurotransmission from one of two inputs converging on a single target shows that more powerful inputs are strongly favoured during synapse elimination — the retention decision is driven by relative, not absolute, efficacy.
- Schafer DP, Lehrman EK, Kautzman AG, Koyama R, Mardinly AR, Yamasaki R, Ransohoff RM, Greenberg ME, Barres BA, Stevens B (2012). **Microglia sculpt postnatal neural circuits in an activity and complement-dependent manner** *Neuron*. PMID:22632727 / doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2012.03.026.
  Supports: Microglia engulf presynaptic inputs during peak retinogeniculate pruning in a manner dependent on neural activity and on the complement receptor 3 / C3 phagocytic signalling pathway; disrupting it produces sustained connectivity deficits — removal is tag-mediated and executed by a delegated non-neuronal agent.
- Holtmaat AJ, Trachtenberg JT, Wilbrecht L, Shepherd GM, Zhang X, Knott GW, Svoboda K (2005). **Transient and persistent dendritic spines in the neocortex in vivo** *Neuron*. PMID:15664179 / doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2005.01.003.
  Supports: Thin spines appear and disappear over days while most thick spines persist for months, and the persistent fraction (lifetime >= 8 days) rises with age from 35% to 73% — retention follows a two-population, probation-then-consolidation policy with link age as a decision variable.
- Südhof TC (2018). **Towards an Understanding of Synapse Formation** *Neuron*. PMID:30359597 / doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2018.09.040.
  Supports: Modern synthesis: trans-synaptic cell-adhesion molecules act as multiple parallel, partly redundant signalling systems that bidirectionally orchestrate synapse formation, restructuring and elimination, rather than as a single lock-and-key formation switch.

## Hebbian plasticity, LTP and LTD

- Bliss TV, Lomo T (1973). **Long-lasting potentiation of synaptic transmission in the dentate area of the anaesthetized rabbit following stimulation of the perforant path** *The Journal of Physiology*. PMID:4727084; DOI:10.1113/jphysiol.1973.sp010273.
  Supports: Discovery of LTP: brief high-frequency stimulation of perforant path fibres potentiates synaptic transmission in dentate granule cells for 30 min to 10 h, via increased synaptic efficiency and increased cell excitability.
- Nowak L, Bregestovski P, Ascher P, Herbet A, Prochiantz A (1984). **Magnesium gates glutamate-activated channels in mouse central neurones** *Nature*. PMID:6320006; DOI:10.1038/307462a0.
  Supports: The NMDA receptor's voltage dependence arises from voltage-dependent Mg2+ block of the channel: at resting potential Mg2+ occludes the pore, and depolarization relieves the block — the biophysical basis of NMDAR coincidence detection.
- Dudek SM, Bear MF (1992). **Homosynaptic long-term depression in area CA1 of hippocampus and effects of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor blockade** *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA*. PMID:1350090; DOI:10.1073/pnas.89.10.4363.
  Supports: 900 pulses at 1-3 Hz induce input-specific, NMDAR-dependent LTD in CA1 while 50 Hz gives potentiation — demonstrating a stimulation-frequency (calcium-level) threshold separating depression from potentiation, as predicted by BCM theory.
- Markram H, Lubke J, Frotscher M, Sakmann B (1997). **Regulation of synaptic efficacy by coincidence of postsynaptic APs and EPSPs** *Science*. PMID:8985014; DOI:10.1126/science.275.5297.213.
  Supports: In paired recordings, the precise timing of postsynaptic action potentials relative to EPSPs determines whether unitary connections are up- or down-regulated, with back-propagating APs serving as the associative signal — foundational STDP demonstration.
- Bi GQ, Poo MM (1998). **Synaptic modifications in cultured hippocampal neurons: dependence on spike timing, synaptic strength, and postsynaptic cell type** *The Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:9852584; DOI:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.18-24-10464.1998.
  Supports: Quantified the asymmetric STDP window: postsynaptic spiking within ~20 ms after presynaptic activation gives LTP, within ~20 ms before gives LTD; both require NMDAR activation, and LTP magnitude depends on initial synaptic strength.
- Frey U, Morris RG (1997). **Synaptic tagging and long-term potentiation** *Nature*. PMID:9020359; DOI:10.1038/385533a0.
  Supports: Late (protein-synthesis-dependent) LTP is gated by a short-lived, protein-synthesis-independent local synaptic tag (decay <3 h) that captures cell-wide plasticity proteins; weak tetanization is consolidated if strong tetanization of another input already triggered protein synthesis.
- Malinow R, Malenka RC (2002). **AMPA receptor trafficking and synaptic plasticity** *Annual Review of Neuroscience*. PMID:12052905; DOI:10.1146/annurev.neuro.25.112701.142758.
  Supports: Review establishing that LTP and LTD are expressed largely through activity-dependent addition and removal of AMPA receptors at synapses, with subunit-specific trafficking rules — synaptic weight as receptor count.
- Lisman J, Yasuda R, Raghavachari S (2012). **Mechanisms of CaMKII action in long-term potentiation** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. PMID:22334212; DOI:10.1038/nrn3192.
  Supports: LTP-induced Ca2+ entry activates CaMKII, which translocates to the stimulated spine, binds NMDA receptors, and phosphorylates AMPAR principal and auxiliary subunits — spine-localized processes that account for the synapse specificity of LTP; CaMKII later plays a structural role in synapse enlargement.
- Bienenstock EL, Cooper LN, Munro PW (1982). **Theory for the development of neuron selectivity: orientation specificity and binocular interaction in visual cortex** *The Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:7054394; DOI:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.02-01-00032.1982.
  Supports: BCM theory: synaptic change depends on instantaneous pre/postsynaptic activity AND a slowly varying time-averaged postsynaptic activity, giving a sliding modification threshold that guarantees convergence to selective (non-saturated) states.
- Abraham WC, Bear MF (1996). **Metaplasticity: the plasticity of synaptic plasticity** *Trends in Neurosciences*. PMID:8658594; DOI:10.1016/s0166-2236(96)80018-x.
  Supports: Defined metaplasticity: prior synaptic or cellular activity persistently changes the ability to induce subsequent LTP/LTD without necessarily changing baseline transmission — a higher-order form of plasticity, partly via altered NMDA receptor function.
- Turrigiano GG, Leslie KR, Desai NS, Rutherford LC, Nelson SB (1998). **Activity-dependent scaling of quantal amplitude in neocortical neurons** *Nature*. PMID:9495341; DOI:10.1038/36103.
  Supports: Discovery of homeostatic synaptic scaling: chronic activity blockade scales up, and chronic hyperactivity scales down, the strength of all of a neuron's excitatory synapses over ~48 h, multiplicatively (each synapse in proportion to its initial strength), stabilizing firing rates during Hebbian modification.
- Turrigiano GG (2008). **The self-tuning neuron: synaptic scaling of excitatory synapses** *Cell*. PMID:18984155; DOI:10.1016/j.cell.2008.10.008.
  Supports: Review of synaptic scaling mechanism: neurons detect deviations in their own firing rate through calcium-dependent sensors and adjust glutamate receptor accumulation globally, forming nested homeostatic loops over different temporal and spatial scales.

## Synaptic tagging and capture

- Frey U, Morris RG (1997). **Synaptic tagging and long-term potentiation** *Nature*. PMID:9020359, DOI:10.1038/385533a0.
  Supports: Founding paper: weak tetanization ordinarily yielding only early-LTP produces protein-synthesis-dependent late-LTP if another input to the same neurons was strongly tetanized; proposes the protein-synthesis-independent synaptic tag that sequesters PRPs; tag decays in <3 h.
- Frey U, Morris RG (1998). **Weak before strong: dissociating synaptic tagging and plasticity-factor accounts of late-LTP** *Neuropharmacology*. PMID:9704995, DOI:10.1016/s0028-3908(98)00040-9.
  Supports: Order-independence and tag decay kinetics: weak tetanus 5 min or 1 h BEFORE strong tetanus still yields late-LTP on the weak pathway (stabilization weak/absent at 2-4 h), dependent on protein synthesis during the strong event — upholding the tag account over a strong-must-precede account.
- Frey U, Morris RG (1998). **Synaptic tagging: implications for late maintenance of hippocampal long-term potentiation** *Trends in Neurosciences*. PMID:9610879, DOI:10.1016/s0166-2236(97)01189-2.
  Supports: Theoretical statement of the tag hypothesis: 'variable persistence' as a function of the neuron's activation history; requirement of aminergic activation for late-LTP; speculation on the cellular identity of the tag and implications for long-term memory.
- Redondo RL, Morris RG (2011). **Making memories last: the synaptic tagging and capture hypothesis** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. PMID:21170072, DOI:10.1038/nrn2963.
  Supports: Modern authoritative review and revision: induction creates only the POTENTIAL for lasting change, not the commitment; the tagged state is dissociable from LTP expression; functional vs structural plasticity differ mechanistically; behavioral studies translate STC to memory consolidation.
- Wang SH, Redondo RL, Morris RG (2010). **Relevance of synaptic tagging and capture to the persistence of long-term potentiation and everyday spatial memory** *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA*. PMID:20962282, DOI:10.1073/pnas.1008638107.
  Supports: Behavioral+electrophysiological parallel: weakly encoded spatial memory decays in 24 h unless novelty exploration occurs ~30 min after encoding; hippocampal D1/D5 blockade or protein-synthesis inhibition within 15 min of exploration abolishes both persistent memory and late-LTP rescue.
- Redondo RL, Okuno H, Spooner PA, Frenguelli BG, Bito H, Morris RG (2010). **Synaptic tagging and capture: differential role of distinct calcium/calmodulin kinases in protein synthesis-dependent long-term potentiation** *Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:20371818, DOI:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3140-09.2010.
  Supports: Molecular dissociation of the two processes: CaMKII inhibition (KN-93) blocks tag SETTING while CaMKK inhibition (STO-609) limits PRP synthesis/availability — local tagging and cell-wide PRP supply run on distinct kinase pathways.
- Moncada D, Viola H (2007). **Induction of long-term memory by exposure to novelty requires protein synthesis: evidence for a behavioral tagging** *Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:17626208, DOI:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1083-07.2007.
  Supports: First demonstration of behavioral tagging: weak inhibitory-avoidance training yielding only STM is consolidated into LTM by exploration of a novel (not familiar) environment close in time to training, dependent on D1/D5 receptors and new hippocampal protein synthesis.
- Ballarini F, Moncada D, Martinez MC, Alen N, Viola H (2009). **Behavioral tagging is a general mechanism of long-term memory formation** *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA*. PMID:19706547, DOI:10.1073/pnas.0907078106.
  Supports: Generality of behavioral tagging: across spatial object recognition, contextual fear, and hippocampus-independent conditioned taste aversion, weak training sets a transient, protein-synthesis-independent, input-specific 'learning tag' and novelty within a critical window around training converts STM to LTM.
- Sajikumar S, Frey JU (2004). **Late-associativity, synaptic tagging, and the role of dopamine during LTP and LTD** *Neurobiology of Learning and Memory*. PMID:15183167, DOI:10.1016/j.nlm.2004.03.003.
  Supports: Cross-tagging: late-LTD is also tagging-governed; process-independent PRPs synthesized by late-LTP in one input transform early-LTD into late-LTD in another input when process-specific tags are set — LTP and LTD share a PRP pool but use distinct tags.
- Sajikumar S, Navakkode S, Sacktor TC, Frey JU (2005). **Synaptic tagging and cross-tagging: the role of protein kinase Mzeta in maintaining long-term potentiation but not long-term depression** *Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:15958741, DOI:10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1104-05.2005.
  Supports: PKMzeta identified as the first LTP-specific PRP: its persistent activity maintains potentiation at both strongly and weakly tetanized (tagged) pathways but not at untetanized ones, and it is required for transforming early- into late-LTP during tagging and cross-tagging while leaving late-LTD intact.
- Govindarajan A, Kelleher RJ, Tonegawa S (2006). **A clustered plasticity model of long-term memory engrams** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. PMID:16791146, DOI:10.1038/nrn1937.
  Supports: Clustered plasticity model: local dendritic translational enhancement combined with synaptic tagging and capture biases long-term engram formation toward synapses within a shared dendritic branch, via bidirectional weight changes among neighboring synapses.
- Okuno H, Akashi K, Ishii Y, Yagishita-Kyo N, Suzuki K, Nonaka M, Kawashima T, Fujii H, Takemoto-Kimura S, Abe M, Natsume R, Chowdhury S, Sakimura K, Worley PF, Bito H (2012). **Inverse synaptic tagging of inactive synapses via dynamic interaction of Arc/Arg3.1 with CaMKIIbeta** *Cell*. PMID:22579289, DOI:10.1016/j.cell.2012.02.062.
  Supports: Inverse tagging: activity-induced Arc targets INACTIVE synapses by high-affinity binding to calmodulin-free CaMKIIbeta, accumulating there and promoting GluA1/AMPA-receptor removal — selectively weakening non-participating synapses in potentiated neurons and preventing undesired enhancement of weak synapses.

## Microglial synaptic pruning

- Stevens B, Allen NJ, Vazquez LE, Howell GR, Christopherson KS, Nouri N, Micheva KD, Mehalow AK, Huberman AD, Stafford B, Sher A, Litke AM, Lambris JD, Smith SJ, John SWM, Barres BA (2007). **The classical complement cascade mediates CNS synapse elimination** *Cell 131(6):1164-78*. PMID:18083105.
  Supports: C1q is expressed by postnatal neurons in response to immature astrocytes, localizes to synapses throughout the postnatal CNS and retina, and C1q- or C3-deficient mice show large sustained failures of retinogeniculate refinement — establishing the 'unwanted synapses are tagged by complement for elimination' model. Also shows C1q is normally downregulated in the adult CNS but is upregulated and synaptically relocalized early in a glaucoma model.
- Schafer DP, Lehrman EK, Kautzman AG, Koyama R, Mardinly AR, Yamasaki R, Ransohoff RM, Greenberg ME, Barres BA, Stevens B (2012). **Microglia sculpt postnatal neural circuits in an activity and complement-dependent manner** *Neuron 74(4):691-705*. PMID:22632727.
  Supports: Microglia engulf RGC presynaptic inputs at peak retinogeniculate pruning (P5, falling sharply by P9); intraocular TTX at P4 makes the silenced eye's inputs preferentially engulfed by P5 while forskolin spares the more active eye; engulfment requires CR3 (CD11b/CD18) and its ligand C3, with CR3/C3 knockouts retaining ~50% of engulfment and showing 1.3x adult synapse density, 1.8x VGlut2 puncta and sustained eye-specific segregation deficits. - Paolicelli RC, Bolasco G, Pagani F, Maggi L, Scianni M, Panzanelli P, Giustetto M, Ferreira TA, Guiducci E, Dumas L, Ragozzino D, Gross CT (2011). **Synaptic pruning by microglia is necessary for normal brain development** *Science 333(6048):1456-8*. PMID:21778362.
  Supports: Microglia actively engulf synaptic material during postnatal development, and CX3CR1 (fractalkine receptor) deficiency produces a transient excess of dendritic spines and immature synaptic connectivity in hippocampus, linking microglial surveillance to synaptic maturation and delayed circuit maturation.
- Nimmerjahn A, Kirchhoff F, Helmchen F (2005). **Resting microglial cells are highly dynamic surveillants of brain parenchyma in vivo** *Science 308(5726):1314-8*. PMID:15831717.

- Wake H, Moorhouse AJ, Jinno S, Kohsaka S, Nabekura J (2009). **Resting microglia directly monitor the functional state of synapses in vivo and determine the fate of ischemic terminals** *J Neurosci 29(13):3974-80*. PMID:19339593.
  Supports: Microglial processes make brief (~5 min) direct contacts with neuronal synapses at a frequency of about once per hour; contact frequency is activity-dependent, reduced by lowering neuronal activity; after transient ischemia contact duration extends to ~1 hour and is frequently followed by disappearance of the presynaptic bouton — establishing dwell time, not contact per se, as the escalation variable.
- Tremblay MÈ, Lowery RL, Majewska AK (2010). **Microglial interactions with synapses are modulated by visual experience** *PLoS Biology 8(11):e1000527*. PMID:21072242.
  Supports: In juvenile visual cortex, microglial processes localize preferentially near small, transiently growing dendritic spines that are typically lost within 2 days; light deprivation makes microglia less motile, shifts their localization toward a subset of larger persistently shrinking spines, increases phagocytic structures and apposition to synaptic clefts, and light re-exposure reverses these behaviors — showing sensory experience modulates surveillance targeting itself.
- Weinhard L, di Bartolomei G, Bolasco G, Machado P, Schieber NL, Neniskyte U, Exiga M, Vadisiute A, Raggioli A, Schertel A, Schwab Y, Gross CT (2018). **Microglia remodel synapses by presynaptic trogocytosis and spine head filopodia induction** *Nature Communications 9(1):1228*. PMID:29581545.
  Supports: Light-sheet imaging with correlative light/electron microscopy shows microglia perform partial phagocytosis (trogocytosis) of presynaptic boutons and axons with no evidence of postsynaptic elimination, and that trogocytosis is NOT reduced in CR3 knockouts (2.3 ± 0.7 vs 1.5 ± 0.6 events/cell per 3 h, p = 0.37). - Miyamoto A, Wake H, Ishikawa AW, Eto K, Shibata K, Murakoshi H, Koizumi S, Moorhouse AJ, Yoshimura Y, Nabekura J (2016). **Microglia contact induces synapse formation in developing somatosensory cortex** *Nature Communications 7:12540*. PMID:27558646.
  Supports: In vivo multiphoton imaging shows microglial contact with dendrites directly induces filopodia formation, restricted to postnatal day 8-10, preceded by contact-induced Ca2+ transients and actin accumulation; genetic ablation of microglia decreases subsequent spine density, functional excitatory synapses and L4-to-L2/3 connectivity — independent in vivo confirmation that microglial contact is a growth stimulus.
- Lehrman EK, Wilton DK, Litvina EY, Welsh CA, Chang ST, Frouin A, Walker AJ, Heller MD, Umemori H, Chen C, Stevens B (2018). **CD47 protects synapses from excess microglia-mediated pruning during development** *Neuron 100(1):120-134.e6*. PMID:30308165.

- Scott-Hewitt N, Perrucci F, Morini R, Erreni M, Mahoney M, Witkowska A, Carey A, Faggiani E, Schuetz LT, Mason S, Tamborini M, Bizzotto M, Passoni L, Filipello F, Jahn R, Stevens B, Matteoli M (2020). **Local externalization of phosphatidylserine mediates developmental synaptic pruning by microglia** *EMBO Journal 39(16):e105380*.   Supports: Exposed phosphatidylserine is a neuronal 'eat-me' signal for pruning: masking accessible PS with Annexin V or removing microglial TREM2 partially prevents synapse elimination in co-culture; PS exposure and engulfment of PS-labeled material occur at hippocampal and retinogeniculate synapses during established pruning windows; C1q-deficient mice show elevated presynaptic PS exposure and reduced PS engulfment, placing PS upstream of complement tagging.
- Gunner G, Cheadle L, Johnson KM, Ayata P, Badimon A, Mondo E, Nagy MA, Liu L, Bemiller SM, Kim KW, Lira SA, Lamb BT, Tapper AR, Ransohoff RM, Greenberg ME, Schaefer A, Schafer DP (2019). **Sensory lesioning induces microglial synapse elimination via ADAM10 and fractalkine signaling** *Nature Neuroscience 22(7):1075-1088*. PMID:31209379.
  Supports: Whisker lesioning drives microglia-mediated cortical synapse elimination that depends on CX3CR1 and on neuron-derived CX3CL1 cleaved by ADAM10 — but explicitly NOT on complement receptor 3 — demonstrating that context-specific, parallel tag protocols converge on the same microglial effector.
- Hong S, Beja-Glasser VF, Nfonoyim BM, Frouin A, Li S, Ramakrishnan S, Merry KM, Shi Q, Rosenthal A, Barres BA, Lemere CA, Selkoe DJ, Stevens B (2016). **Complement and microglia mediate early synapse loss in Alzheimer mouse models** *Science 352(6286):712-716*. PMID:27033548.
  Supports: C1q increases and associates with synapses before overt plaque deposition; inhibiting C1q, C3 or microglial CR3 reduces phagocytic microglia and early synapse loss; adult microglia engulf synaptic material in a CR3-dependent manner when exposed to soluble Aβ oligomers — showing that the developmental pruning pathway causes pathological loss when reactivated outside its developmental window.
- Wilton DK, Dissing-Olesen L, Stevens B (2019). **Neuron-glia signaling in synapse elimination** *Annual Review of Neuroscience 42:107-127*. PMID:31283900.
  Supports: Modern authoritative review synthesizing that glia-mediated synapse elimination is governed by a balance of cues that both promote and inhibit removal of specific connections, and that the same machinery contributes to pathological synapse loss in injury and neurodegeneration — the framing used here for eat-me/don't-eat-me balance and window-scoped competence.

## Astrocytes and the tripartite synapse

- Lehre KP, Danbolt NC (1998). **The number of glutamate transporter subtype molecules at glutamatergic synapses: chemical and stereological quantification in young adult rat brain** *The Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:9786982.
  Supports: Quantifies glial glutamate transporter provisioning: ~12,000 GLT-1 and ~3,200 GLAST molecules per um^3 of CA1 stratum radiatum (membrane densities ~8,500 and ~2,300 per um^2), with total glial transporter equal to 3-5x the glutamate content of one synaptic vesicle from each of all glutamatergic synapses; also that hippocampal synapses are contacted but not fully ensheathed by astroglia, unlike cerebellar molecular layer.
- Diamond JS, Jahr CE (1997). **Transporters buffer synaptically released glutamate on a submillisecond time scale** *The Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:9169528.
  Supports: The fast operation of glutamate transporters is binding/buffering rather than transport: transporters affect cleft glutamate concentration within the first few hundred microseconds of the synaptic response, while the transport cycle itself is too slow to account for the effect - establishing a two-timescale fast-hold / slow-dispose architecture.
- Tanaka K, Watase K, Manabe T, Yamada K, Watanabe M, Takahashi K, et al. (1997). **Epilepsy and exacerbation of brain injury in mice lacking the glutamate transporter GLT-1** *Science*. PMID:9180080.
  Supports: Loss of the principal astrocytic glutamate transporter GLT-1 produces elevated residual brain glutamate, lethal spontaneous seizures and increased susceptibility to acute cortical injury - clearance failure is catastrophic rather than a graceful degradation.
- Oliet SHR, Piet R, Poulain DA (2001). **Control of glutamate clearance and synaptic efficacy by glial coverage of neurons** *Science*. PMID:11340204.
  Supports: The degree of astrocytic coverage of neurons determines glutamate clearance, extracellular glutamate concentration and diffusion, and thereby modulates transmitter release via presynaptic metabotropic glutamate receptors - astrocytic wrapping is a tunable regulator of synaptic efficacy and inter-synapse crosstalk.
- Pellerin L, Magistretti PJ (1994). **Glutamate uptake into astrocytes stimulates aerobic glycolysis: a mechanism coupling neuronal activity to glucose utilization** *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA*. PMID:7938003.
  Supports: Na+-dependent glutamate uptake (not receptor activation) raises intracellular Na+, activating Na+/K+-ATPase and stimulating glycolysis and lactate production in astrocytes - the clearance event is itself the metabolic demand signal, tightly coupling neuronal activity to glucose utilization.
- Suzuki A, Stern SA, Bozdagi O, Huntley GW, Walker RH, Magistretti PJ, Alberini CM (2011). **Astrocyte-neuron lactate transport is required for long-term memory formation** *Cell*. PMID:21376239.
  Supports: Astrocytic glycogenolysis and lactate transport via MCT4/MCT1 (astrocytic) and MCT2 (neuronal) are required for long-term but not short-term memory and for LTP maintenance, and for induction of pCREB, Arc and p-cofilin - the durable consolidation write has a specific energy precondition that the transient write does not.
- Henneberger C, Papouin T, Oliet SHR, Rusakov DA (2010). **Long-term potentiation depends on release of D-serine from astrocytes** *Nature*. PMID:20075918.
  Supports: Clamping Ca2+ in an individual CA1 astrocyte, or depleting its D-serine / disrupting its exocytosis, blocks LTP induction at nearby synapses by reducing NMDAR co-agonist site occupancy, and this is rescued by exogenous D-serine or glycine - a single astrocyte sets a permissive plasticity threshold for many thousands of surrounding synapses.
- Di Castro MA, Chuquet J, Liaudet N, Bhaukaurally K, Santello M, Bouvier D, Tiret P, Volterra A (2011). **Local Ca2+ detection and modulation of synaptic release by astrocytes** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:21909085.
  Supports: Astrocytic processes in adult hippocampus generate two distinct Ca2+ event classes - highly confined (~4 um) millisecond-scale responses to spontaneous single-vesicle release, and larger regional (~12 um) elevations encoding synchronous synaptic events - both GTP/IP3-dependent, and buffering them reduces synaptic transmission reliability.
- Christopherson KS, Ullian EM, Stokes CCA, Mullowney CE, Hell JW, Agah A, Lawler J, Mosher DF, Bornstein P, Barres BA (2005). **Thrombospondins are astrocyte-secreted proteins that promote CNS synaptogenesis** *Cell*. PMID:15707899.
  Supports: Immature but not mature astrocytes secrete thrombospondin-1/-2, which induce ultrastructurally normal synapses that are presynaptically active but postsynaptically SILENT, acting as a permissive switch that times the developmental window for CNS synaptogenesis.
- Kucukdereli H, Allen NJ, Lee AT, Feng A, Ozlu MI, Conatser LM, et al., Barres BA, Eroglu C (2011). **Control of excitatory CNS synaptogenesis by astrocyte-secreted proteins Hevin and SPARC** *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA*. PMID:21788491.
  Supports: Hevin induces excitatory synapse formation while SPARC is not synaptogenic but specifically antagonizes hevin; hevin-null mice have fewer and SPARC-null mice more excitatory synapses, so synapse number is set by the RATIO of a matched agonist/antagonist pair secreted by the same cell.
- Allen NJ, Bennett ML, Foo LC, Wang GX, Chakraborty C, Smith SJ, Barres BA (2012). **Astrocyte glypicans 4 and 6 promote formation of excitatory synapses via GluA1 AMPA receptors** *Nature*. PMID:22722203.
  Supports: Astrocyte-secreted glypican-4/6 make synapses postsynaptically functional by increasing surface level and clustering of the GluA1 AMPA receptor subunit WITHOUT changing total cellular protein level - unsilencing by relocation of existing components; Gpc4-deficient mice show reduced excitatory current amplitude and AMPAR recruitment.
- Chung WS, Clarke LE, Wang GX, Stafford BK, Sher A, Chakraborty C, et al., Smith SJ, Barres BA (2013). **Astrocytes mediate synapse elimination through MEGF10 and MERTK pathways** *Nature*. PMID:24270812.
  Supports: Astrocytes engulf CNS synapses via the MEGF10 and MERTK phagocytic receptors in a strongly neuronal-activity-dependent manner; double-deficient mice fail to refine retinogeniculate connections and retain excess functional synapses, and astrocytes continue engulfing excitatory and inhibitory synapses in the adult brain.
- Lee JH, Kim JY, Noh S, Lee H, Lee SY, Mun JY, Park H, Chung WS (2021). **Astrocytes phagocytose adult hippocampal synapses for circuit homeostasis** *Nature*. PMID:33361813.
  Supports: In adult mouse CA1, astrocytes (not microglia) dominate activity-dependent elimination of excitatory synapses; astrocyte MEGF10 loss causes accumulation of excessive but functionally impaired synapses, defective long-term plasticity and impaired hippocampal memory formation - failing to prune actively degrades circuit function.
- Bushong EA, Martone ME, Jones YZ, Ellisman MH (2002). **Protoplasmic astrocytes in CA1 stratum radiatum occupy separate anatomical domains** *The Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:11756501.
  Supports: Adjacent protoplasmic astrocytes establish primarily exclusive, non-overlapping territories (tiling), and the canonical marker GFAP delineates only ~15% of the astrocyte's total volume, so marker-based images had led to incorrect conclusions about process overlap.
- Haber M, Zhou L, Murai KK (2006). **Cooperative astrocyte and dendritic spine dynamics at hippocampal excitatory synapses** *The Journal of Neuroscience*. PMID:16943543.
  Supports: Astrocytes rapidly extend and retract fine actin-dependent processes to engage and disengage motile dendritic spines; astrocytic process motility on average EXCEEDS that of the spines, changes are coordinated with spine changes, and astrocyte-spine interactions are preferentially stabilized at larger spines - constant undirected exploration with use-dependent commitment.

## Soma, dendrites and axon: division of labour

- London M, Hausser M (2005). **Dendritic computation** *Annual Review of Neuroscience*. PMID:16033324.
  Supports: Dendrites implement linear and nonlinear elementary computations (passive cable filtering, sublinear summation, local nonlinear mechanisms) rather than merely relaying input to the soma; foundational review of the dendrite-as-computer view.
- Stuart GJ, Spruston N (2015). **Dendritic integration: 60 years of progress** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:26605882.
  Supports: Neurons perform multiple passive and active forms of synaptic integration, confirmed in vitro and in vivo including awake animals; dendrites are critical to single-neuron information processing.
- Major G, Larkum ME, Schiller J (2013). **Active properties of neocortical pyramidal neuron dendrites** *Annual Review of Neuroscience*. PMID:23841837.
  Supports: Thin dendrites act as dynamic computational subunits; NMDA receptors provide the major nonlinear depolarizing drive up to full NMDA spikes, so a branch's input-output function is a clustered-input-triggered regenerative event set by network activity.
- Larkum M (2013). **A cellular mechanism for cortical associations: an organizing principle for the cerebral cortex** *Trends in Neurosciences*. PMID:23273272.
  Supports: Coincident input to opposite poles of the pyramidal neuron (distal tuft + perisomatic) robustly triggers an associative dendritic Ca2+ spike mechanism (BAC firing), gated by compartment-targeted inhibition — the coincidence AND gate framing.
- Poirazi P, Brannon T, Mel BW (2003). **Pyramidal neuron as two-layer neural network** *Neuron*. PMID:12670427.
  Supports: A pyramidal cell's firing rate is predicted by a two-layer model: independent sigmoidal subunits corresponding to long thin terminal dendrites, summed and thresholded at trunk/soma — the branch-as-subunit computational abstraction.
- Losonczy A, Makara JK, Magee JC (2008). **Compartmentalized dendritic plasticity and input feature storage in neurons** *Nature*. PMID:18368112.
  Supports: Branch strength potentiation: dendritic spike-to-soma coupling is modified per-branch via NMDAR-dependent regulation of Kv4.2 K+ channels — information storage in branch excitability, mechanistically distinct from synaptic weight change.
- Cichon J, Gan WB (2015). **Branch-specific dendritic Ca2+ spikes cause persistent synaptic plasticity** *Nature*. PMID:25822789.
  Supports: Different motor learning tasks induce Ca2+ spikes on different apical tuft branches in vivo; somatostatin interneurons enforce branch separation, and breaking it causes interference between learned tasks — branch-level memory allocation.
- Kole MH, Ilschner SU, Kampa BM, Williams SR, Ruben PC, Stuart GJ (2008). **Action potential generation requires a high sodium channel density in the axon initial segment** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:18204443.
  Supports: The AIS carries ~50x the Na+ channel density of soma/proximal dendrites (~2500 pS/um^2), anchored to the actin cytoskeleton, and this density is required for action potential initiation — the physical basis of the decision threshold.
- Kole MH, Stuart GJ (2012). **Signal processing in the axon initial segment** *Neuron*. PMID:22284179.
  Supports: The AIS is a dynamic signal-processing unit — clustered voltage-gated channels give it unique electrical properties that regulate synaptic integration, intrinsic excitability, and transmitter release, not just spike initiation.
- Grubb MS, Burrone J (2010). **Activity-dependent relocation of the axon initial segment fine-tunes neuronal excitability** *Nature*. PMID:20543823.
  Supports: Chronic depolarization moves the entire AIS (channels and scaffold) up to 17 um distally in a reversible, Ca2+-channel-dependent manner, changing spike current threshold — homeostatic plasticity of the decision element on an hours-to-days timescale.
- Rasband MN (2010). **The axon initial segment and the maintenance of neuronal polarity** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. PMID:20631711.
  Supports: The AIS maintains neuronal polarity — ion-channel clustering plus a barrier/filter role that preserves axon-vs-dendrite identity throughout life, with disrupted polarity as a common disease component.
- Maday S, Twelvetrees AE, Moughamian AJ, Holzbaur EL (2014). **Axonal transport: cargo-specific mechanisms of motility and regulation** *Neuron*. PMID:25374356.
  Supports: Anterograde transport supplies distal axons with newly synthesized proteins/lipids and synaptic components; retrograde transport removes aging proteins/organelles for degradation and carries neurotrophic and injury-response signaling back to the soma — cargo-specific bidirectional logistics.
- Luo L, O'Leary DD (2005). **Axon retraction and degeneration in development and disease** *Annual Review of Neuroscience*. PMID:16022592.
  Supports: Selective elimination of axons, dendrites, branches, and synapses occurs WITHOUT loss of the parent neuron, via retraction or degeneration — compartment-scoped pruning as a normal developmental and plasticity mechanism.
- Coleman MP, Hoke A (2020). **Programmed axon degeneration: from mouse to mechanism to medicine** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. PMID:32152523.
  Supports: Wallerian degeneration is a locally executed, actively triggered axon-death program centered on loss of somally supplied labile NMNAT2 and activation of the NADase SARM1 — axon survival depends on continuous delivery from the soma, and the program runs independently of soma death.

## Myelination and adaptive myelination

- Gibson EM, Purger D, Mount CW, Goldstein AK, Lin GL, Wood LS, Inema I, Miller SE, Bieri G, Zuchero JB, Barres BA, Woo PJ, Vogel H, Monje M (2014). **Neuronal activity promotes oligodendrogenesis and adaptive myelination in the mammalian brain** *Science 344(6183):1252304*. PMID:24727982.
  Supports: Optogenetic stimulation of premotor cortex in awake mice elicits OPC proliferation, oligodendrogenesis and increased myelination in deep cortical layers and subcortical white matter, and improves motor function of the corresponding limb; epigenetic blockade of oligodendrocyte differentiation prevents the behavioral improvement — establishing that activity instructs myelination of the active circuit and that new myelin is causally necessary for the functional gain.
- McKenzie IA, Ohayon D, Li H, de Faria JP, Emery B, Tohyama K, Richardson WD (2014). **Motor skill learning requires active central myelination** *Science 346(6207):318-322*. PMID:25324381.
  Supports: Conditional deletion of myelin regulatory factor (Myrf) in adult oligodendrocyte precursors blocks production of NEW oligodendrocytes without affecting pre-existing oligodendrocytes or myelin, and this alone prevents mice from mastering a complex running wheel — dissociating 'building new routes' from 'using existing routes' as separable requirements for learning.
- Xiao L, Ohayon D, McKenzie IA, Sinclair-Wilson A, Wright JL, Fudge AD, Emery B, Li H, Richardson WD (2016). **Rapid production of new oligodendrocytes is required in the earliest stages of motor-skill learning** *Nature Neuroscience 19(9):1210-1217*. PMID:27455109.
  Supports: Enpp6+ newly differentiating oligodendrocytes are accelerated within 2.5 h of first exposure to a complex wheel in subcortical white matter and within 4 h in motor cortex, and Myrf deletion impairs learning within the same ~2-3 h window — fixing the commit latency of activity-dependent myelination at hours, with secondary waves of OPC proliferation over the following week.
- Mensch S, Baraban M, Almeida R, Czopka T, Ausborn J, El Manira A, Lyons DA (2015). **Synaptic vesicle release regulates myelin sheath number of individual oligodendrocytes in vivo** *Nature Neuroscience 18(5):628-630*. PMID:25849985.
  Supports: Blocking synaptic vesicle release (tetanus toxin) reduces the number of myelin sheaths made by individual oligodendrocytes during their short formation window, while stimulating neuronal activity increases it — showing that activity sets a per-cell sheath budget in vivo during a bounded, non-renegotiable window.
- Wake H, Lee PR, Fields RD (2011). **Control of local protein synthesis and initial events in myelination by action potentials** *Science 333(6049):1647-1651*. PMID:21817014.
  Supports: Glutamate released from synaptic vesicles along axons promotes myelin induction by nucleating cholesterol-rich signaling domains between oligodendrocyte and axon and increasing LOCAL synthesis of myelin basic protein via Fyn kinase-dependent signaling — the molecular 'tag' that converts a diffuse activity signal into a site-specific ensheathment commitment.
- Hughes EG, Orthmann-Murphy JL, Langseth AJ, Bergles DE (2018). **Myelin remodeling through experience-dependent oligodendrogenesis in the adult somatosensory cortex** *Nature Neuroscience 21(5):696-706*. PMID:29556025.
  Supports: Longitudinal two-photon imaging shows half the final oligodendrocyte complement is generated after 4 months of age; newly integrated oligodendrocytes myelinate unmyelinated and sparsely myelinated axons and are then extremely stable; sensory enrichment increases oligodendrocyte integration but does NOT change the length of existing sheaths — growth is by appending new segments, not editing committed ones.
- Bacmeister CM, Huang R, Osso LA, Thornton MA, Conant L, Chavez AR, Poleg-Polsky A, Hughes EG (2022). **Motor learning drives dynamic patterns of intermittent myelination on learning-activated axons** *Nature Neuroscience 25(10):1300-1313*. PMID:36180791.
  Supports: Myelin plasticity is targeted specifically to learning-activated axons and proceeds in two staged phases: during learning sheaths RETRACT and nodes of Ranvier lengthen; after learning new sheaths are added, increasing continuous myelination. Computational modeling indicates conduction is initially slowed and subsequently sped, and the magnitude/timing of these dynamics correlates with behavioral improvement.
- Etxeberria A, Hokanson KC, Dao DQ, Mayoral SR, Mei F, Redmond SA, Ullian EM, Chan JR (2016). **Dynamic modulation of myelination in response to visual stimuli alters optic nerve conduction velocity** *Journal of Neuroscience 36(26):6937-6948*. PMID:27358452.
  Supports: Long-term monocular deprivation shortens myelin internode lengths in the retinogeniculate pathway and significantly reduces optic nerve conduction velocity; genetically attenuating glutamatergic transmission from retinal ganglion cells phenocopies the change — direct in vivo evidence that experience tunes internode geometry and thereby conduction speed, with glutamate as the instructive signal.
- Ford MC, Alexandrova O, Cossell L, Stange-Marten A, Sinclair J, Kopp-Scheinpflug C, Pecka M, Attwell D, Grothe B (2015). **Tuning of Ranvier node and internode properties in myelinated axons to adjust action potential timing** *Nature Communications 6:8073*. PMID:26305015.
  Supports: In auditory brainstem sound-localization circuits, low-frequency axons have LARGER diameter but SHORTER internodes, and internode length decreases while node diameter increases along distal segments; simulations plus in vitro/in vivo recordings confirm these deviations from standard scaling exist to adjust conduction velocity and ensure precisely timed depolarization of the calyx of Held — myelin geometry optimizes arrival time, not raw speed.
- Pajevic S, Basser PJ, Fields RD (2014). **Role of myelin plasticity in oscillations and synchrony of neuronal activity** *Neuroscience 276:135-147*. PMID:24291730.
  Supports: Quantifies the computational stakes of conduction delay: a 5 ms delay change can flip two coupled oscillators at ~100 Hz from constructive to destructive interference, and delays smaller than 1 ms can change phase by ~30 degrees, significantly affecting signal amplitude — establishing that small myelin-mediated velocity changes are a functionally decisive, nonlinear control variable for network synchrony.
- Steadman PE, Xia F, Ahmed M, Mocle AJ, Penning ARA, Geraghty AC, Steenland HW, Monje M, Josselyn SA, Frankland PW (2019). **Disruption of oligodendrogenesis impairs memory consolidation in adult mice** *Neuron 105(1):150-164.e6*. PMID:31753579.
  Supports: Preventing learning-induced oligodendrogenesis, without affecting existing oligodendrocytes, impairs consolidation of water-maze and contextual-fear memories and blocks the learning-induced increase in hippocampal sharp-wave-ripple / cortical spindle coupling — showing offline replay coordination depends on conduction infrastructure built during the waking learning episode.
- Yeung MSY, Zdunek S, Bergmann O, Bernard S, Salehpour M, Alkass K, Perl S, Tisdale J, Possnert G, Brundin L, Druid H, Frisén J (2014). **Dynamics of oligodendrocyte generation and myelination in the human brain** *Cell 159(4):766-774*. PMID:25417154.
  Supports: Bomb-pulse 14C dating shows human corpus callosum oligodendrocyte number is established in childhood and remains stable thereafter, with an annual exchange of only ~1/300 oligodendrocytes, whereas myelin membrane itself is exchanged at a high rate — the cellular topology is a decades-long commitment while its content is continuously refreshed, and modulation is carried out largely by mature oligodendrocytes.
- McGee AW, Yang Y, Fischer QS, Daw NW, Strittmatter SM (2005). **Experience-driven plasticity of visual cortex limited by myelin and Nogo receptor** *Science 309(5744):2222-2226*. PMID:16195464.
  Supports: Physiological NgR signaling from myelin-derived Nogo, MAG and OMgp consolidates circuitry established during experience-dependent plasticity and terminates the ocular-dominance critical period: NgR-null mice show normal critical-period plasticity but abnormally CONTINUING plasticity at P45 and P120; the same signaling limits functional recovery after trauma — myelin actively purchases stability at the price of rewiring capacity.
- Harris JJ, Attwell D (2012). **The energetics of CNS white matter** *Journal of Neuroscience 32(1):356-371*. PMID:22219296.
  Supports: Cost-benefit analysis of myelination: although the energetic cost of BUILDING myelin could be repaid within months by the reduced ATP cost of action potentials, the standing cost of MAINTAINING the oligodendrocyte resting potential usually outweighs that saving — so myelination dramatically speeds propagation but need not save energy, making speed and metabolic support (not thrift) the purchased goods.

## Sleep replay and systems consolidation

- Wilson MA, McNaughton BL (1994). **Reactivation of hippocampal ensemble memories during sleep** *Science*. PMID:8036517.
  Supports: Foundational evidence for replay: hippocampal place cells co-active during a spatial task show increased co-firing in subsequent slow-wave sleep versus pre-task sleep; cells active but with non-overlapping fields do not; and the effect declines gradually across each post-behavior sleep session. Supports the claim that replay is an experience-tagged, decaying firing bias rather than a stored copy.
- Buzsáki G (2015). **Hippocampal sharp wave-ripple: A cognitive biomarker for episodic memory and planning** *Hippocampus*. PMID:26135716.
  Supports: Mechanism of the replay packet: SPW-Rs are the most synchronous population pattern in the mammalian brain, arise from CA3 recurrent excitation driving a fast CA1 ripple oscillation, have their spike content temporally coordinated by interneurons to replay fragments of waking sequences 'in a compressed format', and transfer that compressed representation to distributed circuits; selective disruption of SPW-Rs interferes with memory.
- Girardeau G, Benchenane K, Wiener SI, Buzsáki G, Zugaro MB (2009). **Selective suppression of hippocampal ripples impairs spatial memory** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:19749750.
  Supports: Causal necessity of replay: closed-loop selective elimination of sharp wave-ripples during post-training consolidation periods impaired performance on a hippocampus-dependent spatial memory task, establishing that SPW-Rs are required for consolidation rather than epiphenomenal.
- Fernández-Ruiz A, Oliva A, Fermino de Oliveira E, Rocha-Almeida F, Tingley D, Buzsáki G (2019). **Long-duration hippocampal sharp wave ripples improve memory** *Science*. PMID:31197012.
  Supports: Ripple duration is the graded control variable and content is not substitutable: ripple durations are skewed, long-duration ripples increase under memory demand, optogenetically prolonging spontaneously initiated ripples improved maze learning, while randomly induced ripples of similar form contained little spatial information and gave no benefit.
- Staresina BP, Bergmann TO, Bonnefond M, van der Meij R, Jensen O, Deuker L, Elger CE, Axmacher N, Fell J (2015). **Hierarchical nesting of slow oscillations, spindles and ripples in the human hippocampus during sleep** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:26389842.
  Supports: The nested timing hierarchy: direct human intracranial recordings show spindles are modulated by the slow-oscillation up-state, and spindles in turn cluster hippocampal ripples in their troughs, providing fine-tuned temporal frames for hippocampo-neocortical information transfer.
- Maingret N, Girardeau G, Todorova R, Goutierre M, Zugaro M (2016). **Hippocampo-cortical coupling mediates memory consolidation during sleep** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:27182818.
  Supports: Causal, enhancing-direction evidence that timing is the mechanism: on a task designed to trigger encoding but not consolidation, timed electrical stimulation reinforcing endogenous coordination between SPW-Rs, cortical delta waves and spindles produced prefrontal network reorganization and high next-day recall, while control rats performed at chance.
- Rothschild G, Eban E, Frank LM (2017). **A cortical-hippocampal-cortical loop of information processing during memory consolidation** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:27941790.
  Supports: Replay content is top-down addressable: patterned auditory-cortex activity precedes and predicts the content of subsequent hippocampal sharp-wave-ripple activity, and ripple content predicts subsequent cortical activity; sounds played during sleep biased cortical patterns which then predicted hippocampal content. Establishes a cortical→hippocampal→cortical query loop rather than a blind hippocampal flush.
- Rasch B, Büchel C, Gais S, Born J (2007). **Odor cues during slow-wave sleep prompt declarative memory consolidation** *Science*. PMID:17347444.
  Supports: Targeted memory reactivation as a cue-keyed API with strict preconditions: an odor present as learning context, re-presented during SWS, improved retention of hippocampus-dependent declarative but not hippocampus-independent procedural memory, was ineffective during REM or wake or when the odor was omitted at learning, and evoked hippocampal fMRI activation.
- Diekelmann S, Born J (2010). **The memory function of sleep** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*. PMID:20046194.
  Supports: Canonical statement of active systems consolidation and its neuromodulatory gate: during SWS, slow oscillations, spindles and ripples — at minimum cholinergic activity — coordinate the reactivation and redistribution of hippocampus-dependent memories to neocortical sites, with SWS supporting system consolidation and REM supporting synaptic consolidation.
- Klinzing JG, Niethard N, Born J (2019). **Mechanisms of systems memory consolidation during sleep** *Nature Neuroscience*. PMID:31451802.
  Supports: The modern synthesis and the nature of the product: active systems consolidation is described as embedded within a process of global synaptic downscaling; repeated hippocampal replay during SWS gradually transforms and integrates representations in neocortical networks; and systems consolidation qualitatively transforms memories into abstracted, gist-like representations.
- Tononi G, Cirelli C (2014). **Sleep and the price of plasticity: from synaptic and cellular homeostasis to memory consolidation and integration** *Neuron*. PMID:24411729.
  Supports: The synaptic homeostasis hypothesis: waking learning requires net synaptic strengthening, which raises energy and supply costs, decreases signal-to-noise ratio and saturates learning; sleep spontaneous activity renormalizes net synaptic strength, and activity-dependent down-selection of synapses itself explains the benefits of sleep for acquisition, consolidation and integration.
- de Vivo L, Bellesi M, Marshall W, Bushong EA, Ellisman MH, Tononi G, Cirelli C (2017). **Ultrastructural evidence for synaptic scaling across the wake/sleep cycle** *Science*. PMID:28154076.
  Supports: Quantitative structural measurement of downscaling with selective sparing: 3D electron microscopy of 6,920 synapses in mouse motor and sensory cortex found the axon-spine interface decreased ~18% after sleep versus wake, proportionally to size (indicative of multiplicative scaling), but the scaling spared synapses that were large and lacked recycling endosomes — implying a distinction between ~80% weaker/plastic and a minority of stronger/stable synapses.
- McClelland JL, McNaughton BL, O'Reilly RC (1995). **Why there are complementary learning systems in the hippocampus and neocortex: insights from the successes and failures of connectionist models of learning and memory** *Psychological Review*. PMID:7624455.
  Supports: The two-store fast-learner/slow-learner architecture: memories are first stored in the hippocampal system; these changes support reinstatement in neocortex; neocortical synapses change a little on each reinstatement; remote memory is the accumulation of those changes. Connectionist models require gradual, interleaved learning to discover ensemble structure, so the hippocampus permits rapid learning of new items without disrupting that structure, and reinstatement interleaves new memories with old.
- Durrant SJ, Taylor C, Cairney S, Lewis PA (2011). **Sleep-dependent consolidation of statistical learning** *Neuropsychologia*. PMID:21335017.
  Supports: Consolidation yields generalization, and slow-wave sleep is the specific carrier: participants consolidating over a night of sleep, or over an afternoon containing a nap, improved significantly more at recognizing NOVEL sequences obeying a learned probabilistic structure than matched wake controls, and polysomnography showed the level of improvement correlated significantly with the amount of slow-wave sleep obtained.

## Engrams

- Josselyn SA, Tonegawa S (2020). **Memory engrams: Recalling the past and imagining the future** *Science*. PMID:31896692.
  Supports: Defines the engram operationally (Semon's original term; a sparse cell subset undergoing persistent chemical/physical change whose reactivation induces retrieval), and reviews the joint role of intrinsic excitability and synaptic plasticity plus the lifetime of an engram — the framing used throughout the summary.
- Reijmers LG, Perkins BL, Matsuo N, Mayford M (2007). **Localization of a stable neural correlate of associative memory** *Science*. PMID:17761885.
  Supports: Introduces the c-fos-driven, doxycycline-gated transgenic tagging system (TetTag) that permanently marks neurons active in a defined window, and shows that basolateral amygdala neurons tagged during fear conditioning are reactivated at retrieval with the reactivated count correlating positively with behavioral memory strength.
- Liu X, Ramirez S, Pang PT, Puryear CB, Govindarajan A, Deisseroth K, Tonegawa S (2012). **Optogenetic stimulation of a hippocampal engram activates fear memory recall** *Nature*. PMID:22441246.
  Supports: Demonstrates SUFFICIENCY: optogenetic reactivation of ChR2-labeled dentate gyrus neurons that were active during fear learning elicits freezing in a different, neutral context; effect absent in non-conditioned and EYFP controls and specific to the fear-associated tagged population.
- Han JH, Kushner SA, Yiu AP, Cole CJ, Matynia A, Brown RA, Neve RL, Guzowski JF, Silva AJ, Josselyn SA (2007). **Neuronal competition and selection during memory formation** *Science*. PMID:17446403.
  Supports: Establishes allocation-by-competition: manipulating CREB function in subsets of lateral amygdala neurons changes the probability that an individual neuron is recruited into the fear memory trace, supporting a competitive selection model based on relative CREB activity at the time of learning.
- Yiu AP, Mercaldo V, Yan C, Richards B, Rashid AJ, Hsiang HL, Pressey J, Mahadevan V, Tran MM, Kushner SA, Woodin MA, Frankland PW, Josselyn SA (2014). **Neurons are recruited to a memory trace based on relative neuronal excitability immediately before training** *Neuron*. PMID:25102562.
  Supports: Identifies intrinsic excitability (not CREB per se) as the allocation variable: several independent methods of raising excitability bias recruitment into the trace and enhance memory formation, and artificial activation of those neurons alone is a sufficient retrieval cue — the basis for the 'relative excitability immediately before encoding' rule.
- Han JH, Kushner SA, Yiu AP, Hsiang HL, Buch T, Waisman A, Bontempi B, Neve RL, Frankland PW, Josselyn SA (2009). **Selective erasure of a fear memory** *Science*. PMID:19286560.
  Supports: Shows the only demonstrated route to true erasure: inducible diphtheria-toxin ablation of CREB-overexpressing (allocated) lateral amygdala neurons after learning permanently blocks expression of that fear memory, whereas ablating a comparable random subset does not.
- Ryan TJ, Roy DS, Pignatelli M, Arons A, Tonegawa S (2015). **Engram cells retain memory under retrograde amnesia** *Science*. PMID:26023136.
  Supports: The storage-vs-access dissociation ('silent engram'): protein-synthesis-inhibitor amnesia removes the increased synaptic strength and spine density of engram cells, yet direct optogenetic activation still retrieves the memory and engram-cell-specific connectivity is retained — supporting connectivity pattern as the storage substrate and synaptic weights as the retrieval/accessibility mechanism.
- Cai DJ, Aharoni D, Shuman T, Shobe J, Biane J, Song W, Wei B, Veshkini M, La-Vu M, Lou J, Flores SE, Kim I, Sano Y, Zhou M, Baumgaertel K, Lavi A, Kamata M, Tuszynski M, Mayford M, Golshani P, Silva AJ (2016). **A shared neural ensemble links distinct contextual memories encoded close in time** *Nature*. PMID:27251287.
  Supports: Establishes the hours-scale linking window: CA1 ensembles for two contexts overlap when acquired within a day but not a week apart; fear transfers between the linked contexts and the first memory strengthens the second only within a day; aged mice with lower excitability lose the overlap, and raising excitability restores linking.
- Rashid AJ, Yan C, Mercaldo V, Hsiang HL, Park S, Cole CJ, De Cristofaro A, Yu J, Ramakrishnan C, Lee SY, Deisseroth K, Frankland PW, Josselyn SA (2016). **Competition between engrams influences fear memory formation and recall** *Science*. PMID:27463673.
  Supports: Shows the excitability-based competitive process is transient and bidirectional: it co-allocates overlapping neuronal populations to memories for events occurring close in time and DISALLOCATES non-overlapping populations for events at distal times — the basis for describing disallocation as an active separation operation.
- Abdou K, Shehata M, Choko K, Nishizono H, Matsuo M, Muramatsu SI, Inokuchi K (2018). **Synapse-specific representation of the identity of overlapping memory engrams** *Science*. PMID:29903972.
  Supports: Demonstrates synapse-specific addressing: engram sharing underlies memory linkage, while synapse-specific plasticity preserves individual memory identity — optogenetic potentiation or depotentiation of the synapses specific to one memory affects only that memory, and complete amnesia for one memory leaves the linked memory in the shared ensemble intact.
- Kitamura T, Ogawa SK, Roy DS, Okuyama T, Morrissey MD, Smith LM, Redondo RL, Tonegawa S (2017). **Engrams and circuits crucial for systems consolidation of a memory** *Science*. PMID:28386011.
  Supports: Shows prefrontal memory engram cells are generated rapidly during initial learning (via hippocampal-entorhinal and basolateral amygdala input) but become functionally mature only with time under hippocampal engram support, while hippocampal engram cells gradually become silent and amygdala engram cells persist — the 'born silent, matures later; consolidation changes which copy is addressable' claim.
- Roy DS, Park YG, Kim ME, Zhang Y, Ogawa SK, DiNapoli N, Gu X, Cho JH, Choi H, Kamentsky L, Martin J, Mosto O, Aida T, Chung K, Tonegawa S (2022). **Brain-wide mapping reveals that engrams for a single memory are distributed across multiple brain regions** *Nature Communications*. PMID:35379803.
  Supports: Supports the 'unified engram complex': characterizing encoding-activated ensembles across 247 regions identified ~117 regions holding engrams for a single contextual fear memory, with brain-wide reactivation at recall, and simultaneous chemogenetic reactivation of multiple engram ensembles producing greater recall than reactivation of a single one.

## Active forgetting, apoptosis and necrosis

- Shuai Y, Lu B, Hu Y, Wang L, Sun K, Zhong Y (2010). **Forgetting is regulated through Rac activity in Drosophila** *Cell 140(4):579-589*. PMID:20178749.
  Supports: Rac activity actively drives both passive memory decay and interference-induced forgetting; inhibiting Rac extends early memory from hours to >1 day, elevated Rac accelerates decay, acquisition is unaffected, and the mechanism is independent of rutabaga adenylyl cyclase. Endogenous Rac activates on different timescales for gradual decay vs acute reversal learning.
- Berry JA, Cervantes-Sandoval I, Nicholas EP, Davis RL (2012). **Dopamine is required for learning and forgetting in Drosophila** *Neuron 74(3):530-542*. PMID:22578504.
  Supports: Ongoing (tonic) activity of MP1/MV1 dopaminergic neurons onto mushroom body neurons bidirectionally sets forgetting rate for both aversive and appetitive memory, and the same transmitter is split across two receptors on engram cells: dDA1 for acquisition, DAMB for forgetting.
- Davis RL, Zhong Y (2017). **The Biology of Forgetting - A Perspective** *Neuron 95(3):490-503*. PMID:28772119.
  Supports: Taxonomy of active forgetting into neurogenesis-based, interference-based and intrinsic forgetting; the best-characterized intrinsic pathway runs dopamine from 'forgetting cells' onto engram cells to Rac1/cofilin and actin/spine remodelling; intrinsic forgetting may be the brain's default state, constantly competing with consolidation.
- Liu Y, Du S, Lv L, Lei B, Shi W, Tang Y, Wang L, Zhong Y (2016). **Hippocampal Activation of Rac1 Regulates the Forgetting of Object Recognition Memory** *Current Biology 26(17):2351-2357*. PMID:27593377.
  Supports: Rac1-dependent active forgetting is conserved in mammals: hippocampal dominant-negative Rac1 extends object recognition memory from <72 h to >72 h, Rac1 activation accelerates decay within 24 h, and interference-induced forgetting correlates with Rac1 activation and is blocked by inhibiting it.
- Awasthi A, Ramachandran B, Ahmed S, Benito E, Shinoda Y, Nitzan N, Heukamp A, Rannio S, Martens H, Barth J, Burk K, Wang YT, Fischer A, Dean C (2019). **Synaptotagmin-3 drives AMPA receptor endocytosis, depression of synapse strength, and forgetting** *Science 363(6422):eaav1483*. PMID:30545844.
  Supports: Synaptotagmin-3 at postsynaptic endocytic zones is a Ca2+ sensor that internalizes AMPA receptors; Syt3 knockout mice learn normally but exhibit a lack of forgetting, and LTP fails to decay — isolating receptor removal as the effector primitive of synaptic decay.
- Migues PV, Liu L, Archbold GEB, Einarsson EÖ, Wong J, Bonasia K, Ko SH, Wang YT, Hardt O (2016). **Blocking Synaptic Removal of GluA2-Containing AMPA Receptors Prevents the Natural Forgetting of Long-Term Memories** *Journal of Neuroscience 36(12):3481-3494*. PMID:27013677.
  Supports: Blocking GluA2/AMPAR removal during the retention interval preserves long-term memories without affecting acquisition, and critically also blocks contextual fear generalization and prevents spontaneous recovery of extinguished fear — direct evidence that the deletion process is what produces generalization.
- Akers KG, Martinez-Canabal A, Restivo L, Yiu AP, De Cristofaro A, Hsiang HL, Wheeler AL, Guskjolen A, Niibori Y, Shoji H, Ohira K, Richards BA, Miyakawa T, Josselyn SA, Frankland PW (2014). **Hippocampal neurogenesis regulates forgetting during adulthood and infancy** *Science 344(6184):598-602*. PMID:24812394.
  Supports: Continuous addition of new dentate granule cells remodels hippocampal circuits and degrades established memories: increasing neurogenesis after encoding induces forgetting in adult mice, decreasing it during infancy mitigates infantile amnesia, and precocial species lacking high postnatal neurogenesis show no infantile amnesia unless neurogenesis is increased.
- Wang C, Yue H, Hu Z, Shen Y, Ma J, Li J, Wang XD, Wang L, Sun B, Shi P, Wang L, Gu Y (2020). **Microglia mediate forgetting via complement-dependent synaptic elimination** *Science 367(6478):688-694*. PMID:32029629.
  Supports: Microglial phagocytosis of synaptic components underlies forgetting of remote memories in healthy adult mice; depleting microglia or inhibiting phagocytosis prevents forgetting and engram-cell dissociation, and expressing the complement inhibitor CD55 specifically in engram cells shows the process is complement- and activity-dependent.
- Anderson MC, Bjork RA, Bjork EL (1994). **Remembering can cause forgetting: retrieval dynamics in long-term memory** *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition 20(5):1063-1087*. PMID:7931095.
  Supports: Retrieval practice of some category members causes lasting impairment of unpracticed members of the same category; the effect survives control of output interference (implying active retrieval-based suppression lasting 20+ min) and is restricted to high-frequency members — inhibition targeted at strong competitors.
- Ryan TJ, Frankland PW (2022). **Forgetting as a form of adaptive engram cell plasticity** *Nature Reviews Neuroscience 23(3):173-186*. PMID:35027710.
  Supports: Forgetting is proposed to be circuit remodelling that switches engram cells from an accessible to an inaccessible state rather than trace deletion; rates are modulated by mismatch between expectations and the environment, and disease states can hijack these natural forgetting mechanisms.
- Richards BA, Frankland PW (2017). **The Persistence and Transience of Memory** *Neuron 94(6):1071-1084*. PMID:28641107.
  Supports: Transience is functionally equivalent to regularization: it enhances flexibility by reducing the influence of outdated information and prevents overfitting to specific past events, thereby promoting generalization; memory's goal is optimizing decision-making rather than transmitting information through time.
- Kerr JFR, Wyllie AH, Currie AR (1972). **Apoptosis: a basic biological phenomenon with wide-ranging implications in tissue kinetics** *British Journal of Cancer 26(4):239-257*. PMID:4561027.
  Supports: Apoptosis is an actively programmed, controlled cell-deletion mechanism complementary to mitosis, proceeding in two discrete stages: condensation and breakup into membrane-bound, ultrastructurally well-preserved apoptotic bodies, then uptake by other cells and lysosomal degradation inside the ingesting cell — orderly containment in contrast to uncontrolled autolysis.
- Nagata S (2018). **Apoptosis and Clearance of Apoptotic Cells** *Annual Review of Immunology 36:489-517*. PMID:29400998.
  Supports: A caspase cascade culminating in caspase-3 both kills the cell and posts recruitment and 'eat-me' signals (phosphatidylserine exposure via scramblase/flippase control) for macrophage engulfment; apoptosis is noninflammatory/silent whereas necroptosis and pyroptosis inflame, and failed engulfment leads to secondary necrosis releasing DAMPs and driving autoimmune disease. ~10^10-10^11 cells are cleared daily.
- Li Z, Jo J, Jia JM, Lo SC, Whitcomb DJ, Jiao S, Cho K, Sheng M (2010). **Caspase-3 activation via mitochondria is required for long-term depression and AMPA receptor internalization** *Cell 141(5):859-871*. PMID:20510932.
  Supports: The apoptotic executioner machinery is used sub-lethally and locally for plasticity: NMDAR stimulation transiently activates caspase-3 in dendrites without cell death, caspase-3 is required for LTD and AMPAR internalization (LTP is normal in caspase-3 knockouts), and LTD is blocked by XIAP or Bcl-xL overexpression and by a caspase-3-resistant Akt1 mutant.
- Oppenheim RW (1991). **Cell death during development of the nervous system** *Annual Review of Neuroscience 14:453-501*. PMID:2031577.
  Supports: Developmental programmed cell death eliminates roughly half of the neurons initially produced; neurons are overproduced and compete for limiting quantities of target-derived neurotrophic factor, with losers dying, so population size is matched to target-field requirements by elimination rather than by precise initial specification. (Abstract not indexed in PubMed; content confirmed via the Annual Reviews record and secondary literature.)

## Corrections (binding)

- Drescher U, Kremoser C, Handwerker C, Loschinger J, Noda M, Bonhoeffer F 1995 (PMID:7634326) — VERIFIED RECORD, MISATTRIBUTED CLAIM. Record is correct (Cell 82(3):359-70). But the sweep uses it for 'each axon stops where repulsion crosses its own receptor-scaled threshold — an address computed from relative, not absolute, values, so the map rescales automatically.' The paper reports the opposite of the specificity half: RAGS repelled temporal AND nasal axons, and the authors state an additional activity is needed for nasotemporal specificity. The relative/ratio result is Reber 2004 (PMID 15483613); the matched-gradient result is Cheng & Flanagan 1995 (PMID 7634327).
- 'RAGS/ephrin-A2' (growth-cones prose) — PROBABLE NAMING ERROR. RAGS was subsequently identified as ephrin-A5; ephrin-A2 is ELF-1, from Cheng & Flanagan 1995 (PMID 7634327), a different paper in the same Cell issue. Note that NLM indexes BOTH papers under the MeSH term 'Ephrin-A2', so PubMed metadata will not resolve this — it needs a human check against current nomenclature before the mapping inherits the wrong ligand name.
- Sajikumar S, Navakkode S, Sacktor TC, Frey JU 2005 (PMID:15958741) — RECORD FINE, CLAIM NOW CONTESTED. The sweep states as fact that PKMzeta 'maintains potentiation.' Volk et al. 2013 (PMID 23283174) found normal LTP and normal hippocampal learning/memory in PKC-zeta/PKM-zeta knockouts, and critically that ZIP — the inhibitor all the original evidence relies on — still reverses LTP in those knockouts, meaning ZIP's effects are PKMzeta-independent. Flag as unsupported-as-stated rather than as a citation error.
- Govindarajan A, Kelleher RJ, Tonegawa S 2006 (PMID:16791146) — CORRECT RECORD, WRONG EVIDENCE CLASS. Verified as Nat Rev Neurosci 7(7):575-83, article type Review; it proposes the clustered plasticity model. The sweep cites it for an empirical finding about branch-level capture competition and time-clustered co-allocation. Substitute or add Govindarajan et al. 2011, Neuron 69(1):132 (PMID 21220104).
- Frey U, Morris RG 1998, TINS (PMID:9610879) — REVIEW CITED AS PRIMARY SOURCE. Verified correct (Trends Neurosci 21(5):181-8, article type Review). It is cited for the requirement of dopamine D1/D5 activation for PRP synthesis; the abstract only says it 'outline[s] the requirement for aminergic activation.' The primary D1/D5 late-LTP results (Frey et al. 1990/1991; Huang & Kandel 1995) are not in the citation list, so the neuromodulatory gate — a load-bearing element of the design mapping — has no primary source.
- Mitchison T, Kirschner M 1988 (PMID:3078414) — REVIEW-ONLY SOURCING FOR A LOAD-BEARING MECHANISM. Verified correct (Neuron 1(9):761-72) but article type is Review, as is the co-cited Lowery & Van Vactor 2009. The molecular clutch is the central actuator mechanism in growth-cones and no primary measurement of clutch engagement or of the flow-versus-traction relationship is cited.
- Weinhard L et al. 2018 (PMID:29581545) — INFERENCE FROM A NULL RESULT. Record verified (Nat Commun 9(1):1228). The quoted trogocytosis statistics (2.3 +/- 0.7 vs 1.5 +/- 0.6 events/cell/3 h, p = 0.37) are not in the abstract and could not be confirmed here; more importantly, a non-significant difference is being used to assert that trogocytosis is 'a pathway separate from complement-tagged engulfment.' That is absence of evidence, not evidence of separation — the two-pathway architecture claim should be softened.
- engrams area — TWO UNCITED RESULTS. 'Ramirez 2013' (false memory installation) and 'Zhou 2009' (excitability as the mediating variable for CREB-driven allocation) are named in the prose but appear in no citation record, so neither PMID, year, nor journal can be checked and the claims are unverifiable as written.
- Dudek SM, Bear MF 1992 (PMID:1350090) — MINOR PARAMETER IMPRECISION. The abstract gives 'nine hundred pulses at 1-3 Hz'; the sweep renders this as '900 pulses at 1-3 Hz for ~15 min.' 900 pulses take 15 min only at 1 Hz (5 min at 3 Hz). Trivial, but the sweep presents these as exact induction parameters.
